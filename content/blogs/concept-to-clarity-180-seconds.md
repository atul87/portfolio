A promising idea is not enough on its own. You also need to explain the problem, show why it matters, and make the proposed solution easy to understand. I was reminded of this while participating with my teammate Mehul Kumar Das in Allenhouse Institute of Technology's *Idea Sprint — From Concept to Clarity in 180 Seconds*, held on National Engineers' Day on 15 September 2025.

We presented **CivicConnect**, a concept for crowdsourced civic issue reporting and resolution. The project was awarded **Second Runner-Up**. It was a proud moment, and it also gave me a useful perspective on how early-stage ideas should be communicated.

## A short pitch forces useful questions

When time is limited, it becomes harder to hide behind general statements. You have to answer a few direct questions:

- What problem are we trying to solve?
- Who experiences that problem?
- What does the proposed solution let them do?
- Why is this approach worth considering?

For CivicConnect, the problem was familiar: residents may notice potholes, garbage accumulation, or broken streetlights, but reporting and tracking those issues can be difficult. The core idea was to give citizens a way to report issues through a mobile experience and help the responsible teams track, route, and resolve them.

That explanation works because the problem and the intended workflow are connected. It is more concrete than saying only that an app will make a city “smart.”

## Separate the problem from the feature list

One easy mistake in project presentations is to start listing technologies or features before explaining the need. A technology list may show what the team has used, but it does not automatically show why anyone should care.

I find it helpful to frame an idea in this order:

1. **Context:** Describe the real situation in ordinary language.
2. **Problem:** Explain what is difficult, slow, confusing, or missing today.
3. **Solution:** Show the simplest useful workflow.
4. **Value:** Explain how that workflow could help the intended users.
5. **Next validation step:** State what should be tested before making bigger claims.

The final item is important. An idea sprint is a chance to communicate a concept, not proof that the concept has already created measurable real-world impact. A credible presentation distinguishes what exists today from what the team hopes to validate next.

## Teamwork is part of the engineering process

Building and presenting an idea with a teammate requires shared understanding. If two people explain the problem differently, the audience may struggle to see the purpose of the solution. Aligning on the user, the main workflow, and the most important message helps the presentation feel coherent.

It also helps to practice likely questions. What happens when a report is incomplete? How would a team avoid duplicate entries? How would a citizen know whether a report was received? These questions reveal gaps that may not be obvious when the team is focused only on the pitch.

The award was a welcome recognition, and I am grateful to Allenhouse, the jury, and the mentors who made the platform possible. Just as importantly, the experience reinforced how much a team can learn by putting an idea in front of other people and listening to their questions.

## A simple framework I will keep using

For future project presentations, I want to keep returning to three habits:

- **Make the problem specific.** Use an example that an audience can picture.
- **Explain the workflow, not just the technology.** Show how a person moves from a problem to an outcome.
- **Be honest about the evidence.** Distinguish the prototype, the proposed behavior, and results that have actually been measured.

Three minutes is a short time, but it is enough to communicate a clear idea when the team understands what matters most.

**Innovation is not only about having a new idea. It is also about making the idea understandable enough that other people can evaluate it, challenge it, and help improve it.**
